How I work

Rules I work by

1 / 7

“What problem are we solving?”

Before we argue about a feature, a bug, or a roadmap, I ask what problem it solves. Half the arguments end right there. Either the problem doesn’t exist yet and we’ve collectively fallen in love with building something for the sake of building it, or the solution doesn’t actually solve the problem we have.

2 / 7

Bad news is where strategy starts.

Strategy is how we respond when things go wrong: name the real challenge, then design a way through it. So when someone brings me bad news, my next question is “okay, what do we do with it?”

Credit to Richard Rumelt’s Good Strategy Bad Strategy, one of the few business books that actually changed how I work.

3 / 7

Stuck and exhausted? Decide something and test it.

If a project has worn everyone out, trying one thing quickly beats three more months of circling. Even a wrong attempt teaches you more than another meeting.

4 / 7

One page beats five meetings.

It’s fine to come to a meeting not knowing what you want. Then it’s a brainstorm, and we work out the problem together. But if the meeting has to end with decisions, one page of research beforehand saves hours of arguing.

5 / 7

“Impossible” usually means “we don’t know enough yet.”

Every tech team’s favourite first reaction to a new idea is “that’s technically impossible.” I get it, I’ve said it too. But after a bit of research, it often turns out to be doable, and the drama becomes an interesting engineering challenge.

6 / 7

Once we decide, we pull in one direction.

Argue as much as you like before the decision. After it, a team half-heartedly pulling three ways loses to a team fully committed to a slightly imperfect plan.

7 / 7

If the result isn’t what I wanted, I probably didn’t explain it.

So before anyone starts, I say what I expect, by when, what “good” looks like, and how deep to go. A quick experiment and six months of R&D are very different jobs, and nobody can guess which one I had in mind.
